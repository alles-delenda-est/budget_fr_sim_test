## Texte de Gemini

# Evidence Review: Long-Run Supply Effects of French Fiscal and Structural Measures (v2.0)

**Target Use:** French Public-Finance Simulator — Supply-Side Specification  
**Functional Form:**
$$y_S(t) = \sigma(j) \times a(j) \times \text{clamp}\!\left(\frac{t - t_0(j)}{L(j) - t_0(j)},\, 0,\, 1\right)$$
where $y_S(t)$ is the permanent change in the **level of real GDP (in %)**, $a(j) > 0$ is the measure's size in the direction stated in each heading, $\sigma(j)$ is the long-run GDP-level effect per unit, $L(j)$ is the full-adjustment horizon in years, and $t_0(j)$ is an optional initial grace delay (default $t_0 = 0$) before the supply effect begins to materialize.

**Methodological & Epistemic Rules Applied:**
1. **Reading status:** Every empirical study cited below was verified via published abstracts, bibliographic metadata, and official institutional evaluation syntheses rather than full-text PDF reading in this session; accordingly, each empirical entry is flagged **`[abstract / synthesis only]`**.
2. **Models vs. empirical evidence:** Outputs from calibrated DSGE, CGE, spatial-equilibrium, or microsimulation models are explicitly flagged **`[model result, not empirical evidence]`** and are never used as primary justification for a non-zero $\sigma$.
3. **Conservative default:** Whenever empirical confidence is **Low** (due to publication bias, tax-avoidance/rent-sharing confounders, theoretical ambiguity, or lack of quasi-experimental identification), the central recommendation is set to **$\sigma = 0.00$**.
4. **Budget-closure convention:** All standalone fiscal $\sigma(j)$ are defined relative to a lump-sum financing benchmark so that balanced-budget combinations in an additive simulator automatically net the supply effect of the spending/cut instrument against the supply effect of the financing instrument.

## Part I — Fiscal Measures

### 1. Raising the legal retirement age by one year (France: 2010 and 2023 reforms)

1. **Mechanism**: Raising the statutory minimum eligibility age (*âge d'ouverture des droits*, AOD) increases aggregate labour supply both directly by retaining older workers past the old retirement threshold and upstream by lengthening the career horizon for workers aged 55–59.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Rabaté, S., & Rochut, J. (2020 [online 2019]).** "Employment and substitution effects of raising the statutory retirement age in France." *Journal of Pension Economics and Finance*, 19(3), 293–308. DOI: [10.1017/S1474747218000392](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1474747218000392) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (2010 reform raising AOD from 60 to 62, cohorts born 1951–1952), difference-in-differences (DiD) on CNAV administrative data. **Quantity estimated**: Claiming a pension at age 60 fell by 48 percentage points (pp); age-60 employment rate rose by **+21 pp**, alongside significant spillovers into unemployment and disability/sickness schemes (~20% fiscal offset).
   - **Dubois, Y., & Koubi, M. (2017).** "La réforme des retraites de 2010 : quel impact sur l'activité des séniors ?" *Économie & Prévision*, 211–212(2), 61–90 (INSEE Working Paper [G2016/08](https://www.insee.fr/fr/publications-et-services/docs_doc_travail/G2016-08.pdf)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, 2008–2014 Labour Force Survey, cohort DiD. **Quantity estimated**: Labour-force participation at affected ages rose by **+19 to +23 pp** and the employment rate rose by **+16 pp**, primarily by retaining workers already employed at ages 58–59.
   - **Hairault, J.-O., Sopraseuth, T., & Langot, F. (2010).** "Distance to Retirement and Older Workers' Employment: The Case for Delaying the Retirement Age." *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 8(5), 1034–1076. DOI: [10.1111/j.1542-4774.2010.tb00547.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1542-4774.2010.tb00547.x) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, Labour Force Survey panel and life-cycle search model. **Quantity estimated**: Demonstrates a statistically significant upstream "distance-to-retirement effect" boosting employment rates at ages 55–59 when the statutory retirement age is delayed.
   - *Ex-ante benchmark on the 2023 reform* **`[model result, not empirical evidence]`**: The 2023 reform (AOD 62→64) was suspended in 2026 until 2028 by the LFSS 2026, precluding ex-post evaluation. Ex-ante DG Trésor projections (Combier, 2026, *Trésor-Éco* n° 406; Ecalle & Redoulès, 2023, *Rexecode Repères* n° 5) simulated **+300,000 jobs** and **+1.1% of GDP** by 2030 for the 2-year AOD increase plus Touraine acceleration.
3. **Conversion**: A single French birth cohort near age 60–63 comprises ~750,000–800,000 individuals. A +16 to +21 pp employment gain per affected cohort year implies **+125,000 to +165,000 employed workers** per +1 year of AOD (+0.41% to +0.54% of France's ~30.4 million total employment). Assuming long-run capital adjustment keeps $K/Y$ constant (unit elasticity of GDP to efficiency-weighted labour) and a relative productivity/hours weight of 0.85–1.0 for retained seniors yields **+0.35% to +0.54% of GDP** per year of AOD (with diminishing returns beyond age 64 as baseline pre-retirement employment is lower).
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **+0.25 / +0.45 / +0.60** (% of GDP per **+1 year of legal retirement age**; equivalent to ~**+1.30% of GDP per 1% of GDP** ex-ante pension savings), with lag **$L = 6$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **High** (converging French quasi-experimental studies on administrative and survey data for the 2010 reform).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: Both Rabaté & Rochut (2020) and Dubois & Koubi (2017) document a *sas de précarité*: individuals already out of employment at 58–59 do not return to work when AOD rises, shifting instead into unemployment, sick leave, and disability benefits (+13 pp probability of precariousness at age 60).

### 2. Cutting employer social contributions on low wages (near the minimum wage), per 1% of GDP

1. **Mechanism**: Because France's binding statutory minimum wage (SMIC) prevents downward real wage adjustment, targeted employer payroll-tax cuts directly reduce low-skilled labour costs and expand labour demand.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Kramarz, F., & Philippon, T. (2001).** "The impact of differential payroll tax subsidies on minimum wage employment." *Journal of Public Economics*, 82(1), 115–146. DOI: [10.1016/S0047-2727(00)00137-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0047-2727(00)00137-7) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, 1990–1998 longitudinal *Enquête Emploi* panel comparing minimum-wage cost increases vs. tax-subsidy decreases. **Quantity estimated**: Asymmetric transition probabilities into/out of non-employment; strong negative employment elasticity when minimum-wage labour costs rise, moderate positive job creation when subsidies reduce costs.
   - **Cahuc, P., Carcillo, S., & Le Barbanchon, T. (2019).** "The Effectiveness of Hiring Credits." *The Review of Economic Studies*, 86(2), 593–626. DOI: [10.1093/restud/rdy011](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdy011) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, 2008–2009 hiring credit for firms <10 employees on wages $\le 1.6\text{ SMIC}$; quasi-experimental DiD + structural search-and-matching model on exhaustive administrative data. **Quantity estimated**: Strong positive employment creation and zero wage pass-through due to binding SMIC rigidity, though structural counterfactuals show permanent broad credits have smaller multipliers than temporary hiring credits.
   - **L'Horty, Y., Martin, P., & Mayer, T. (2019).** "Baisses de charges : stop ou encore ? / The French Policy of Payroll Tax Reductions." *Notes du Conseil d'Analyse Économique*, n° 49, 1–12 ([cae-eco.fr](https://www.cae-eco.fr/); synthesizing Crépon & Desplatz, 2001, *Économie et Statistique*, 348, 3–34) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: French policy evaluation synthesis. **Quantity estimated**: Confirms robust employment creation for exemptions targeted between $1.0$ and $1.6\text{ SMIC}$, with steep attenuation above $1.6\text{ SMIC}$.
3. **Conversion**: A 1% of GDP cut (~€29 bn) concentrated on wages $\le 1.6\text{ SMIC}$ (covered payroll ~€290 bn) lowers targeted labour costs by ~10%. Using a consensus low-wage labour-demand elasticity of $-0.5$ to $-0.9$ (net of macroeconomic displacement) yields **+150,000 to +260,000 jobs** (+0.50% to +0.86% of total employment). Weighting by marginal productivity near the SMIC (~0.65 of the economy-wide mean wage) and assuming constant long-run $K/Y$ yields **+0.35% to +0.60% of GDP**. *(Saturation warning: since 2019, general URSSAF contributions at $1.0\text{ SMIC}$ are already near zero; further cuts must push exemptions up the wage distribution where effectiveness falls, whereas hiking employer SSCs at the SMIC carries the full negative $-\sigma$).*
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **+0.20 / +0.45 / +0.80** (% of GDP per **1% of GDP** ex-ante cut), with lag **$L = 5$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **Medium** (strong converging French evidence on the SMIC-rigidity channel, tempered by saturation at $1.0\text{ SMIC}$ today and inter-firm substitution).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: Sterdyniak (2002, *Revue de l'OFCE* n° 81) critiques micro-econometric estimates (such as Crépon & Desplatz, 2001) for ignoring macro financing and inter-firm market-share stealing; Bozio, Breda & Guillot (2023, *JPubE*) highlight that steep phase-outs between $1.0$ and $1.6\text{ SMIC}$ create high marginal tax wedges ("low-wage traps") that penalize skill upgrading.

### 3. Cutting employer contributions across all wages, per 1% of GDP

1. **Mechanism**: Across-the-board employer payroll-tax cuts aim to lower aggregate unit labour costs and boost export competitiveness and labour demand, but above the SMIC they are largely absorbed into pre-tax wages via rent-sharing and collective bargaining.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Carbonnier, C., Malgouyres, C., Py, L., & Urvoy, C. (2022).** "Who benefits from tax incentives? The heterogeneous wage incidence of a tax credit." *Journal of Public Economics*, 206, 104577. DOI: [10.1016/j.jpubeco.2021.104577](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2021.104577) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, 2013–2015 evaluation of the CICE (~0.9% of GDP tax credit on wages up to $2.5\text{ SMIC}$) on exhaustive matched employer–employee data. **Quantity estimated**: Roughly **50% of the tax cut was passed through into wages** of incumbent skilled workers via collective rent-sharing, with **no robust aggregate employment gains** detected in companion France Stratégie/LIEPP evaluations.
   - **Malgouyres, C., & Mayer, T. (2018).** "Exports and labor costs: evidence from a French policy." *Review of World Economics*, 154(3), 429–454. DOI: [10.1007/s10290-018-0320-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10290-018-0320-x) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, customs and firm-level microdata evaluation of the CICE. **Quantity estimated**: Tests the trade/competitiveness channel directly and finds **noisy, statistically insignificant effects of CICE exposure on firm exports**.
   - **Bozio, A., Breda, T., Grenet, J., & Guillouzouic, A. (2024).** "Does Tax-Benefit Linkage Matter for the Incidence of Payroll Taxes?" *PSE Working Paper / Oxford Academic* ([parisschoolofeconomics.eu](https://www.parisschoolofeconomics.eu/)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, administrative data across six payroll and income tax reforms. **Quantity estimated**: Employer SSCs *with* tax-benefit linkage shift **100% onto workers' wages** in the long run (zero employment effect), while unlinked SSC cuts above the SMIC experience partial wage capture.
3. **Conversion**: Direct French quasi-experimental evaluations of broad cuts extending well above the SMIC (CICE up to $2.5\text{ SMIC}$) detect **zero statistically significant employment or export effect** and heavy wage capture. Positive output claims rely on either liquidity-constrained firm channels or calibrated CGE models (e.g., IMF WP 14/113 `[model result, not empirical evidence]`, simulating ~+0.35% GDP per 1% of GDP cut).
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **0.00 / 0.00 / +0.35** (% of GDP per **1% of GDP** ex-ante cut), with lag **$L = 5$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **Low** (credible French micro-econometric evidence on CICE finds no employment or export response and substantial wage capture; central $\sigma = 0.00$ recommended).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Saez, E., Schoefer, B., & Seim, D. (2019)** ("Payroll Taxes, Firm Behavior, and Rent Sharing: Evidence from a Young Workers' Tax Cut in Sweden," *American Economic Review*, 109(5), 1717–1763, DOI: [10.1257/aer.20171937](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20171937)) find that youth payroll-tax cuts in Sweden expanded employment and capital in credit-constrained firms via a firm-liquidity windfall channel, providing an empirical basis for the $+0.35$ upper bound.

### 4. Cutting the corporate income tax rate by one point

1. **Mechanism**: Lowering the statutory corporate income tax (CIT) rate reduces the user cost of corporate capital, theoretically encouraging domestic investment, FDI inflows, and capital deepening per worker.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Gechert, S., & Heimberger, P. (2022).** "Do corporate tax cuts boost economic growth?" *European Economic Review*, 147, 104157. DOI: [10.1016/j.euroecorev.2022.104157](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2022.104157) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Global/OECD meta-regression analysis of **441 estimates from 42 empirical studies**. **Quantity estimated**: Real GDP growth; documents strong publication bias favouring growth-enhancing CIT cuts. **Once corrected for publication bias, the hypothesis of a zero effect on GDP growth cannot be rejected**.
   - **Fuest, C., Peichl, A., & Siegloch, S. (2018).** "Do Higher Corporate Taxes Reduce Wages? Micro Evidence from Germany." *American Economic Review*, 108(2), 393–418. DOI: [10.1257/aer.20130570](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20130570) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Germany, 20-year panel of 6,800 municipal business-tax changes using event-study and DiD on linked employer–employee data. **Quantity estimated**: Roughly **50% of the corporate tax burden falls on wages** via collective rent-sharing, while indirect output/investment effects are small.
   - **Bach, L., Bozio, A., Fabre, B., Guillouzouic, A., Leroy, C., & Malgouyres, C. (2019; updated 2021/2023).** *Évaluation d'impact de la fiscalité des dividendes* (Rapport IPP n° 25 / France Stratégie Comité d'évaluation des réformes de la fiscalité du capital, [shs.hal.science/halshs-02514741v1](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-02514741v1)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (2013 and 2018 PFU capital-tax reforms), DiD on exhaustive firm–household tax registers. **Quantity estimated**: Large dividend payout and income-shifting responses, **but zero statistically significant effect on physical investment among existing French firms**.
3. **Conversion**: In France, a 1 percentage point cut in the statutory CIT rate costs ~0.08%–0.10% of GDP ex-ante. Gechert & Heimberger (2022) show the bias-corrected macro effect is indistinguishable from zero ($\sigma = 0.00$). Taking standard user-cost-of-capital channels as an upper bound (1 pp CIT cut lowers user cost by ~1%, corporate capital elasticity $-0.5$, corporate capital share ~0.25) yields at most **+0.12% to +0.15% of GDP** per 1 pp rate cut (note: targeted accelerated depreciation allowances, per Zwick & Mahon, 2017, *AER*, have much larger investment bang-for-buck than statutory rate cuts, which bestow windfalls on existing capital).
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **0.00 / 0.00 / +0.15** (% of GDP per **1 percentage point CIT rate cut**; equivalent to **0.00 / 0.00 / +1.65** per **1% of GDP** ex-ante cost), with lag **$L = 7$ years** ($t_0 = 1$).
5. **Confidence**: **Low** (comprehensive 2022 meta-analysis rejects a robust non-zero GDP effect after correcting for publication bias; central $\sigma = 0.00$ recommended).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: Cross-country panel regressions (**Arnold, J. M., et al., 2011**, *The Economic Journal*, 121(550), F59–F80) and historical US innovation panels (**Akcigit, Grigsby, Nicholas & Stantcheva, 2022**, *QJE*, 137(1), 329–385) find negative effects of corporate taxation on GDP per capita and corporate patenting.

### 5. Cutting personal income tax at the top (top 1% marginal rates), including the migration channel

1. **Mechanism**: Lowering top marginal personal income tax (PIT) rates increases the net-of-tax rate $(1 - \tau)$, potentially raising intensive-margin effort, entrepreneurship, and net inflows of high-productivity mobile earners/inventors.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Guillot, M. (2021 [WP 2018]).** "Who Paid the French 75% Tax on Millionaires? Effects on top wage earners and their employers." *PSE / IPP Working Paper* ([ipp.eu](https://www.ipp.eu/)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, 2013–2014 temporary 75% marginal tax on gross wages >€1m, DiD on exhaustive fiscal/social administrative records. **Quantity estimated**: Employers bore 80% of the tax; pre-tax labour income elasticity to $(1 - \tau)$ was **0.30**, driven by **pure intertemporal optimization/time-shifting, with zero detectable migration response**.
   - **Kleven, H. J., Landais, C., Saez, E., & Schultz, E. (2014).** "Migration and Wage Effects of Taxing Top Earners: Evidence from the Foreigners' Tax Scheme in Denmark." *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 129(1), 333–378. DOI: [10.1093/qje/qjt033](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjt033) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Denmark (post-1991 preferential flat-tax scheme for high-paid foreign arrivals), RDD and DiD on population registers. **Quantity estimated**: Large migration elasticity (**1.5 to 2.0**) wrt $(1-\text{ATR})$ *specifically for foreign arrivals*, **negative** effect on individual pre-tax wages (bargaining), and **zero spillovers on native top earners' wages**.
   - **Akcigit, U., Baslandze, S., & Stantcheva, S. (2016).** "Taxation and the International Mobility of Inventors." *American Economic Review*, 106(10), 2930–2981. DOI: [10.1257/aer.20150237](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20150237) (synthesized in **Kleven, Landais, Muñoz & Stantcheva, 2020**, *JEP*, 34(2), 119–142) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: International patent panel across OECD countries. **Quantity estimated**: Superstar *foreign* inventors have a net-of-tax migration elasticity of ~1.0, whereas *domestic* inventors' migration elasticity is tiny (**~0.03**).
3. **Conversion**: With France's top marginal rate (IR + CEHR + CSG) near 55% ($1 - \tau = 0.45$), a 1 pp cut raises $(1 - \tau)$ by $2.2\%$. Even assuming a generous *real* (non-avoidance, non-rent-shifting) combined intensive-margin + domestic-migration elasticity of $0.15$, and a top-1% labour value-added share of ~6.5% of GDP, the implied GDP effect is $0.022 \times 0.15 \times 6.5\% \approx \mathbf{+0.02\%}$ **of GDP** per 1 pp top rate cut (ex-ante cost ~0.025% of GDP). Moreover, Bach, Bozio, Guillouzouic & Malgouyres (2023, *IPP Note* n° 92) show top French earners retain income inside holding companies, muting direct PIT exposure.
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **0.00 / 0.00 / +0.03** (% of GDP per **1 percentage point cut in the top 1% marginal PIT rate**; equivalent to **0.00 / 0.00 / +1.20** per **1% of GDP** ex-ante cost), with lag **$L = 5$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **Low** (French administrative evidence shows top taxable-income responses reflect timing/avoidance and bargaining rather than real output, and native migration elasticities are near zero; central $\sigma = 0.00$ recommended).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Piketty, T., Saez, E., & Stantcheva, S. (2014)** ("Optimal Taxation of Top Labor Incomes: A Tale of Three Elasticities," *AEJ: Economic Policy*, 6(1), 230–271) find zero correlation between large post-1970 top marginal tax cuts and real GDP per capita growth across OECD economies, attributing top-income elasticities to rent-seeking/bargaining rather than productive supply.

### 6. Raising the standard VAT rate by one point (as financing for a labour-tax cut, i.e. "TVA sociale")

1. **Mechanism**: Shifting taxation from domestic payrolls onto destination-based consumption ("fiscal devaluation") taxes imports while exempting exports and non-labour income, reducing domestic unit labour costs in the short run until nominal wages and CPI-indexed transfers adjust.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Benzarti, Y., & Carloni, D. (2019).** "Who Really Benefits from Consumption Tax Cuts? Evidence from a Large VAT Reform in France." *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 11(1), 38–63. DOI: [10.1257/pol.20170504](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20170504) (paired with **Benzarti, Carloni, Harju & Kosonen, 2020**, *Journal of Political Economy*, 128(12), 4438–4474) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (2009 restaurant VAT cut) and all EU VAT reforms (1996–2015), DiD on firm and price microdata. **Quantity estimated**: VAT hikes pass through strongly and persistently into consumer prices (2× to 4× more than VAT cuts), rapidly feeding into CPI inflation.
   - **Benzarti, Y., & Tazhitdinova, A. (2021).** "Do Value-Added Taxes Affect International Trade Flows? Evidence from 30 Years of Tax Reforms." *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 13(4), 469–489. DOI: [10.1257/pol.20190498](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20190498) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: European Union (30 years of VAT reforms), quasi-experimental panel estimation on bilateral trade flows. **Quantity estimated**: Directly tests whether VAT changes alter net exports and finds **very small, near-zero effects of VAT reforms on international trade flows**.
   - **Farhi, E., Gopinath, G., & Itskhoki, O. (2014).** "Fiscal Devaluations." *The Review of Economic Studies*, 81(2), 725–760. DOI: [10.1093/restud/rdt036](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdt036) **`[theoretical / DSGE model result, not empirical evidence]`**. **Quantity analysed**: Proves that a revenue-neutral VAT hike + uniform payroll-tax cut replicates a nominal devaluation **only in the short/medium run under sticky nominal wages**; in the long run, once nominal wages and prices adjust, real competitiveness effects vanish.
3. **Conversion**: In France, +1 point of the standard VAT rate raises ~0.45% of GDP. In the **long run**, automatic legal indexation of the SMIC and pensions to CPI, combined with wage bargaining (Bozio et al., 2024; Carbonnier et al., 2022), erodes the real labour-cost wedge unless transfer recipients permanently absorb purchasing-power losses. *(Double-counting warning: the $\sigma$ below applies to the **combined revenue-neutral TVA sociale package** per +1 pp standard VAT; if the simulator already credits the payroll-tax cut via Measure 2 or 3, the standalone +1 pp VAT hike leg must be set to $\sigma_{\text{VAT-only}} \in [-0.20, 0.00]$, central $0.00$ or $-0.10$, to avoid double-counting).*
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **-0.10 / 0.00 / +0.20** (% of GDP per **+1 percentage point standard VAT financing an equal-yield broad payroll-tax cut**), with lag **$L = 5$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **Low** (long-run theoretical neutrality under CPI indexation of SMIC/pensions and near-zero empirical trade-flow effects in Benzarti & Tazhitdinova, 2021; central $\sigma = 0.00$ recommended).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: Cross-country OECD panel regressions (Arnold et al., 2011) find that revenue-neutral shifts from income/payroll taxation to consumption taxation correlate positively with long-run GDP per capita.

### 7. Cutting the research tax credit (Crédit d'impôt recherche)

1. **Mechanism**: Reducing the *Crédit d'impôt recherche* (CIR, ~0.27% of GDP or ~€7.5–8 bn) raises the user cost of private R&D, lowering business R&D investment, patenting, and long-run total factor productivity (TFP)—with sharply different effects depending on whether cuts target the 30% SME/base rate or the €100m ceiling for large corporate groups.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Commission nationale d'évaluation des politiques d'innovation — CNEPI (Lallement, R., & Harfi, M., 2021).** *Évaluation du Crédit d'impôt recherche — Rapport CNEPI 2021*. France Stratégie ([strategie.gouv.fr](https://www.strategie.gouv.fr/publications/evaluation-credit-dimpot-recherche-rapport-cnepi-2021)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Synthesis of econometric evaluations (DiD / event studies on the 2008 CIR reform). **Quantity estimated**: R&D input additionality multiplier of **~1.0** (€1 of CIR generates ~€1 of private R&D); **statistically significant positive effects on value added, patents, and productivity for SMEs/TPEs, but no statistically significant economic performance effect for large enterprises**.
   - **Mulkay, B., & Mairesse, J. (2018).** *Nouveaux résultats sur l'impact du Crédit d'impôt recherche*. Rapport MESRI ([enseignementsup-recherche.gouv.fr](https://www.enseignementsup-recherche.gouv.fr/); extending Mulkay & Mairesse, 2013, *Oxford Economic Papers*, 65(3), 746–766) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, 1994–2013 panel of 7,400 firms (35,000 observations), structural factor-demand estimation of the 2008 CIR reform. **Quantity estimated**: Long-run elasticity of R&D capital to user cost of $-0.50$, implying a long-run R&D additionality multiplier converging to **0.90**.
   - **Bach, L., Bozio, A., Guillouzouic, A., Malgouyres, C., & Serrano-Velarde, N. (2021).** *Les impacts du crédit d'impôt recherche sur la performance économique des entreprises*. Rapport IPP n° 33 ([ipp.eu](https://www.ipp.eu/)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, quasi-experimental evaluation of the 2008 CIR expansion on firm tax and balance-sheet microdata. **Quantity estimated**: Positive real output/productivity impacts on SMEs, **but no statistically significant effect on value added or productivity for large corporate groups** (which absorb ~two-thirds of CIR expenditure).
3. **Conversion**: Total CIR expenditure is ~0.27% of GDP. Standardizing to **per 1% of GDP cut** ($a(7) \le 0.27$): if €1 of CIR generates €0.90 of reported R&D, but ~30%–50% goes into higher researcher wages (**Goolsbee, A., 1998**, *AER*, 88(2), 298–302) or large groups with low marginal output additionality, effective productivity-enhancing R&D falls by ~0.40% to 0.70% of GDP per 1% of GDP cut. Given French business R&D ($BERD \approx 1.45\%$ of GDP) and an output elasticity to R&D capital of ~0.08, steady-state TFP/GDP falls by **-1.2% per 1% of GDP cut** on the general/SME base rate (i.e., **-0.32% of GDP** if the entire 0.27%-of-GDP CIR were abolished), vs. **0.00%** if only the large-group ceiling is lowered. *(By contrast, DG Trésor, 2021, Trésor-Éco n° 290 `[model result, not empirical evidence]`, reports macro-model simulations of the 2008 CIR reform of +0.5% to +1.6% of GDP in QUEST, Mésange, and NEMESIS).*
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**:
   - **7a (General / SME base-rate cut)**: **-2.50 / -1.20 / -0.40** (% of GDP per **1% of GDP cut in CIR**, $a \le 0.27$), lag **$L = 12$ years** ($t_0 = 2$).
   - **7b (Lowering the €100m ceiling on large corporate groups only)**: **-0.50 / 0.00 / 0.00** (% of GDP per **1% of GDP cut**, $a \le 0.12$), lag **$L = 12$ years** ($t_0 = 2$).
5. **Confidence**: **Medium** for 7a (converging French micro-econometric evidence of ~0.9–1.0 R&D additionality and SME value-added gains); **Low** for 7b (null large-group output findings in Bach et al., 2021).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: Goolsbee (1998, *AER*) shows inelastic scientist/engineer supply causes R&D subsidies to inflate researcher wages rather than real R&D output; CNEPI (2021) and Bach et al. (2021) find zero detectable productivity gains among large corporate recipients.

### 8. Changes in public investment, per 1% of GDP

1. **Mechanism**: Productive public capital (transport, energy grids, digital and local core infrastructure) enters private production as an unpaid complementary input, raising private marginal productivities and crowding in private capital in the long run.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Bom, P. R. D., & Ligthart, J. E. (2014).** "What Have We Learned from Three Decades of Research on the Productivity of Public Capital?" *Journal of Economic Surveys*, 28(5), 889–916. DOI: [10.1111/joes.12037](https://doi.org/10.1111/joes.12037) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: OECD/industrial countries, meta-regression analysis of **578 estimates across 68 studies**, correcting for publication bias and simultaneity. **Quantity estimated**: Private output elasticity of public capital ($\varepsilon_{Y, K_G}$) averages **0.106** overall, **0.122** in the long run for central-government public capital, and **~0.19** for regional/local core infrastructure.
   - **Charnoz, P., Lelarge, C., & Trevien, C. (2018).** "Communication Costs and the Internal Organisation of Multi-Plant Businesses: Evidence From the Impact of the French High-Speed Rail." *The Economic Journal*, 128(610), 949–994. DOI: [10.1111/ecoj.12592](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12592) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, exhaustive INSEE corporate-group microdata exploiting the staggered roll-out of the French TGV network. **Quantity estimated**: Travel-time reductions causally increased functional specialization between headquarters and remote plants, raising affiliate production employment and corporate group profitability.
   - **Ramey, V. A. (2021 [WP 2020]).** "The Macroeconomic Consequences of Infrastructure Investment." In E. L. Glaeser & J. M. Poterba (Eds.), *Economic Analysis and Infrastructure Investment* (pp. 219–268). University of Chicago Press. DOI: [10.7208/chicago/9780226800615.003.0005](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226800615.003.0005) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: US/OECD macroeconomic synthesis and bias diagnostics. **Quantity estimated**: Places realistic output elasticities of public capital at **0.05 to 0.12**, showing long-run multipliers shrink when initial public capital is already near its social optimum.
3. **Conversion**: In France, general-government gross fixed capital formation ($I_G$) is ~4.0%–4.2% of GDP ($K_G \approx 60\%$ of GDP). In steady state ($\Delta K_G / K_G = \Delta I_G / I_G$), a permanent **+1% of GDP** increase in public investment expands steady-state public capital by **~+24%–25%**. With $Y = A K_G^\theta K_P^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ and private capital adjusting to keep $K_P / Y$ constant ($\alpha \approx 0.30$), $\Delta \ln Y = \frac{\theta}{1-\alpha} \Delta \ln K_G$. Discounting Bom & Ligthart's $\theta = 0.106$ for France's dense baseline infrastructure ($\theta \in [0.025, 0.09]$, central $0.05$, per Ramey, 2021) yields **+0.80% to +3.10% of GDP** (central **+1.80%**).
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **+0.80 / +1.80 / +3.10** (% of GDP per **+1% of GDP** permanent change in public investment), with lag **$L = 15$ years** and construction grace delay **$t_0 = 3$ years**.
5. **Confidence**: **Medium** (large meta-analytic evidence correcting for publication bias plus French TGV micro-evidence, tempered by diminishing returns in infrastructure-rich France).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: Ramey (2021) demonstrates that when public capital is near or above its golden-rule optimum, financing non-core infrastructure yields near-zero or negative net private output effects.

### 9. Changes in education spending, per 1% of GDP (long-run human-capital channel)

1. **Mechanism**: Sustained increases in school and university resources can raise cognitive skills and educational attainment if effectively targeted, increasing cohort labour productivity as educated cohorts gradually replace older workers over 35–40 years.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Bénabou, R., Kramarz, F., & Prost, C. (2009).** "The French *Zones d'Éducation Prioritaire*: Much Ado About Nothing?" *Economics of Education Review*, 28(3), 345–356. DOI: [10.1016/j.econedurev.2008.04.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2008.04.005) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (1982–1992 ZEP priority-school funding programme), DiD and IV on national student panels. **Quantity estimated**: Extra funding for teacher bonuses and instructional hours had **zero statistically significant effect on student test scores, grade repetition, or diploma attainment**.
   - **DEPP — Direction de l'évaluation, de la prospective et de la performance (2026).** *Réduction de la taille de classe en éducation prioritaire : que nous apprennent les données de la DEPP ?* Synthèse de la DEPP n° 8, février 2026 (synthesizing **Andreu et al., 2021** and **Murat, F., & Rocher, T., 2026**, Document de travail DEPP n° 2026.E02, [education.gouv.fr](https://www.education.gouv.fr/)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (2017–2021 reform halving Grade 1–2 CP/CE1 class sizes from ~24 to ~12 in REP/REP+ priority schools), longitudinal panel evaluation. **Quantity estimated**: Significant positive effect on French and math scores at the end of CE1, **but no durable effect on student test scores at entry into 6e (middle school)** in metropolitan France.
   - **Jackson, C. K., Johnson, R. C., & Persico, C. (2016).** "The Effects of School Spending on Educational and Economic Outcomes: Evidence from School Finance Reforms." *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 131(1), 157–218. DOI: [10.1093/qje/qjv036](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv036) (alongside **Maurin, É., & McNally, S., 2008**, *Journal of Labor Economics*, 26(1), 1–35 on returns to French higher education) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: US court-mandated state school-finance reforms, event-study/IV. **Quantity estimated**: +10% per-pupil spending across all 12 school years raised adult wages by **~+7%** among low-income students.
3. **Conversion**: French public education spending is ~5.2% of GDP, so +1% of GDP is a ~19% budget increase. While US court-mandated reforms (Jackson et al., 2016) and French higher-education quantity shocks (Maurin & McNally, 2008) imply an upper-bound steady-state productivity gain of **+2.5% of GDP** once all cohorts in the workforce are replaced, direct French evaluations of K–12 resource increases (Bénabou et al., 2009; DEPP, 2026) find zero or fading cognitive effects by middle school. Weighting these yields a conservative central estimate of **+0.80% of GDP** for targeted human-capital investment (and **0.00%** for untargeted across-the-board spending), materializing only after an 8-year schooling delay over a 35-year generational horizon.
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **0.00 / +0.80 / +2.50** (% of GDP per **+1% of GDP** permanent change in education spending; use **0.00** if untargeted), with lag **$L = 35$ years** and schooling grace delay **$t_0 = 8$ years**.
5. **Confidence**: **Low–Medium** (direct French quasi-experimental evaluations of ZEP and REP+ class-size reductions show null or fading medium-run achievement gains, tempering positive US and higher-education estimates).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Hanushek, E. A. (2003)** ("The Failure of Input-Based Schooling Policies," *The Economic Journal*, 113(485), F64–F98, DOI: [10.1111/1468-0297.00099](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0297.00099)) documents across hundreds of studies that unconditional financial resource increases without institutional/incentive reforms fail to raise student achievement.

### 10. Tightening unemployment-insurance rules (duration, replacement rate), with reference to the French 2019–2023 reforms

1. **Mechanism**: Reducing the daily benefit replacement rate (*salaire journalier de référence*, SJR) or shortening potential benefit duration (PBD) increases job-search intensity and accelerates exit from unemployment, raising aggregate employment while potentially increasing short-term contract churn.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Le Bihan, M., & Fabre, B. (2026).** "Spreading Out the Payment of Unemployment Benefits: The Impact on Transitions to Employment / Étaler le versement des allocations chômage : quels effets sur le retour à l'emploi ?" *Économie et Statistique / Economics and Statistics*, 550, 3–22. DOI: [10.24187/ecostat.2026.550.2145](https://doi.org/10.24187/ecostat.2026.550.2145) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (October 2021 SJR reform cutting daily benefits by ~16%–22% while extending entitlement spans for intermittent workers), quasi-experimental DiD on Unédic administrative records. **Quantity estimated**: Return to employment within 6 months rose by **+3.2 to +6.8 percentage points** for affected claimants (~25%–30% of inflows), driven mainly by fixed-term contracts lasting <6 months.
   - **Le Barbanchon, T., Rathelot, R., & Roulet, A. (2019).** "Unemployment insurance and reservation wages: Evidence from administrative data." *Journal of Public Economics*, 171, 1–17. DOI: [10.1016/j.jpubeco.2017.05.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2017.05.002) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (2009 UI affiliation/duration reform), DiD and RDD on Pôle Emploi registers. **Quantity estimated**: Elasticity of actual benefit duration wrt potential benefit duration (PBD) is **0.30**, while reservation-wage elasticity is **precisely zero**.
   - **Schmieder, J. F., von Wachter, T., & Bender, S. (2016).** "The Effect of Unemployment Benefits and Nonemployment Durations on Wages." *American Economic Review*, 106(3), 739–777. DOI: [10.1257/aer.20141566](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20141566) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Germany (1987–2004), age-threshold RDD on social-security registers. **Quantity estimated**: Shorter UI duration significantly reduces non-employment spells and raises re-employment wages by limiting skill depreciation during long unemployment spells.
3. **Conversion**: Standardizing the unit to **a 10% tightening of UI generosity** (either a 10% cut in PBD—such as ~40% of the February 2023 French 25% duration cut—or an equivalent 10% replacement-rate cut, saving ~0.14%–0.15% of GDP ex-ante): an individual duration elasticity of $0.30$ (Le Barbanchon et al., 2019), discounted by ~35% for search congestion/displacement (**Marinescu, I., & Skandalis, D., 2021**, *QJE*, 136(2), 887–931) and ~50% ARE coverage among ILO unemployed, lowers the structural unemployment rate by **~0.10 to 0.22 pp** (+30,000 to +65,000 jobs). Weighting marginal re-entrants (who disproportionately enter on short temporary contracts, per Le Bihan & Fabre, 2026) at ~0.70 of the average economy-wide wage and keeping $K/Y$ constant yields **+0.07% to +0.22% of GDP** per 10% tightening (central **+0.12%**, or **+0.80% of GDP per 1% of GDP** ex-ante UI benefit cut).
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **+0.05 / +0.12 / +0.22** (% of GDP per **10% reduction in UI PBD or replacement rate**; equivalent to **+0.35 / +0.80 / +1.40** per **1% of GDP** ex-ante UI spending cut), with lag **$L = 3$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **High** (converging French quasi-experimental studies on both the 2009 and 2019–2021 Unédic reforms).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Nekoei, A., & Weber, A. (2017)** (*American Economic Review*, 107(2), 527–561) find in Austrian RDD data that shorter UI benefits reduce subsequent re-employment wages by ~0.5% per 30% cut by inducing worse job matches; Marinescu (2017, *JPubE*) shows micro-elasticities overstate macro employment gains during slack labour markets due to job-rationing spillovers.

## Part II — Structural Measures (No Direct Budget Line)

### 11. Labour-market reforms of the Hartz type (Germany 2003–2005), and their transferability to France

1. **Mechanism**: Restructuring public employment services (Hartz III) and merging long-term unemployment assistance with means-tested welfare (Hartz IV) aim to raise job-matching efficiency and lower reservation wages, expanding equilibrium employment.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Launov, A., & Wälde, K. (2013).** "Estimating Incentive and Welfare Effects of Nonstationary Unemployment Benefits." *International Economic Review*, 54(4), 1159–1198. DOI: [10.1111/iere.12032](https://doi.org/10.1111/iere.12032) (paired with **Launov & Wälde, 2016**, "The employment effect of reforming a public employment agency," *European Economic Review*, 84, 140–164, DOI: [10.1016/j.euroecorev.2015.08.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2015.08.008)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Germany (2003–2005 Hartz III & IV reforms), structural search-and-matching model estimated via maximum likelihood on microdata. **Quantity estimated**: Hartz IV benefit cuts reduced Germany's equilibrium unemployment rate by only **-0.1 pp**, whereas Hartz III public employment agency reorganization reduced unemployment by **-0.7 to -0.9 pp** (~20% of the post-2005 decline).
   - **Bradley, J., & Kügler, A. (2019).** "Labor market reforms: An evaluation of the Hartz policies in Germany." *European Economic Review*, 113, 108–135. DOI: [10.1016/j.euroecorev.2018.12.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2018.12.008) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Germany (2000–2010), structural equilibrium search model estimated on IAB administrative panel data across all four Hartz phases. **Quantity estimated**: The Hartz reforms shortened unemployment duration, **reduced wages**, and **did not significantly raise aggregate employment**.
   - **Dustmann, C., Fitzenberger, B., Schönberg, U., & Spitz-Oener, A. (2014).** "From Sick Man of Europe to Economic Superstar: Germany's Resurgence after Reunification." *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 28(1), 167–188. DOI: [10.1257/jep.28.1.167](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.28.1.167) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: Germany (1990–2012), institutional and wage-distribution synthesis. **Quantity estimated**: Attributes Germany's competitiveness recovery primarily to **pre-2003 firm-level collective bargaining decentralization and works-council wage moderation** rather than the 2003–2005 Hartz laws.
3. **Conversion**: Discounting Launov & Wälde's (2013, 2016) combined Hartz III+IV estimate (-0.8 to -1.0 pp unemployment) by 50% for **limited transferability to France**—because France's binding national SMIC prevents bottom-decile real wage falls, and because France already enacted parts of the package via the 2008 Pôle Emploi merger, 2019–2023 UI reforms, and 2023 *France Travail* law—implies at most **+0.3% to +0.6% of GDP**, while Bradley & Kügler (2019) imply **0.00%**.
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **0.00 / 0.00 / +0.60** (% of GDP per **full Hartz III–IV style reform package**), with lag **$L = 6$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **Low** (sharp disagreement across German econometric studies, pre-2003 bargaining confounders in Dustmann et al., 2014, and binding SMIC limits in France; central $\sigma = 0.00$ recommended).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Burda, M. C., & Seele, S. (2020)** (*German Economic Review*, 21(2), 145–177, DOI: [10.1515/ger-2019-0069](https://doi.org/10.1515/ger-2019-0069)) use shift-share supply–demand diagnostics to argue a post-2003 low-wage labour-supply shock did occur, and calibrated macro models (**Krebs & Scheffel, 2013**, *IMF Economic Review*, 61(4), 664–701 `[model result, not empirical evidence]`) simulate a -1.4 pp fall in structural unemployment from Hartz IV.

### 12. Easing dismissal rules (employment protection legislation), including the French 2017 ordinances

1. **Mechanism**: Lowering firing costs, capping judicial uncertainty (*barème prud'homal*), and relaxing firm-size threshold obligations simultaneously increase job destruction and job creation—leaving net aggregate employment theoretically ambiguous—while potentially raising TFP through faster inter-firm labour reallocation and reduced firm-size distortions.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Fraisse, H., Kramarz, F., & Prost, C. (2015).** "Labor Disputes and Job Flows." *ILR Review*, 68(5), 1043–1077. DOI: [10.1177/0019793915591989](https://doi.org/10.1177/0019793915591989) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (1996–2003), instrumental-variables estimation exploiting local lawyer-supply shocks across *Conseils de prud'hommes* on exhaustive individual labour-court records and firm job flows. **Quantity estimated**: Higher dismissal litigation costs **significantly reduce both job destruction and job reallocation**, while the net effect on aggregate employment growth is **near zero or slightly positive** (mirroring the official **France Stratégie, 2021** evaluation of the 2017 *ordonnances Travail*, which found lower court litigation from the *barème prud'homal* but **no identifiable macro employment or CDI hiring effect**).
   - **Garicano, L., Lelarge, C., & Van Reenen, J. (2016).** "Firm Size Distortions and the Productivity Distribution: Evidence from France." *American Economic Review*, 106(11), 3439–3479. DOI: [10.1257/aer.20130232](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20130232) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (exhaustive manufacturing firm panels, FICUS), structural estimation of size-contingent labour regulations at the **50-employee threshold**. **Quantity estimated**: Regulatory obligations at 50 employees act as a 2.3% variable labour tax above 49 workers, generating an output/TFP misallocation loss of **1.3% of GDP** under flexible wages and up to **3.4% of GDP** under downward real wage rigidity.
   - **Bassanini, A., Nunziata, L., & Venn, D. (2009).** "Job protection legislation and productivity growth in OECD countries." *Economic Policy*, 24(58), 349–402. ### 13. Easing land-use and building-permit constraints (housing supply)

1. **Mechanism**: Relaxing local zoning, floor-area-ratio caps (*Plan local d'urbanisme*, PLU), commercial entry barriers, and building-permit restrictions in high-productivity metropolitan areas (especially Île-de-France) expands residential and commercial floor space, lowering housing prices and allowing workers to relocate toward higher-TFP urban agglomerations.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Combes, P.-P., Duranton, G., & Gobillon, L. (2019).** "The Costs of Agglomeration: House and Land Prices in French Cities." *The Review of Economic Studies*, 86(4), 1556–1589. DOI: [10.1093/restud/rdy063](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdy063) (paired with **Combes, Duranton, Gobillon, Puga & Roux, 2012**, *Econometrica*, 80(6), 2543–2594) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (exhaustive parcel-level land transactions, housing costs, and worker wage panels across French urban areas). **Quantity estimated**: Urban agglomeration productivity elasticity wrt city density/size is **0.03 to 0.05** (net of worker skill sorting), while urban house-price elasticity wrt population is ~0.21 (and land-price elasticity ~0.72), showing regulatory land-supply constraints choke off positive net agglomeration gains.
   - **Bertrand, M., & Kramarz, F. (2002).** "Does Entry Regulation Hinder Job Creation? Evidence from the French Retail Industry." *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 117(4), 1369–1413. DOI: [10.1162/003355302320935052](https://doi.org/10.1162/003355302320935052) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (1975–1998 departmental commercial zoning approvals under the *Loi Royer*), IV exploiting local political shifts. **Quantity estimated**: Relaxing French commercial zoning entry restrictions would have increased retail employment by **~7% to 10%** and lowered consumer prices.
   - **Chapelle, G., Eyméoud, J.-B., & Wolf, C. (2023 [WP 2018]).** "Land-use regulation and housing supply elasticity: evidence from France." *Sciences Po LIEPP / THEMA Working Paper* `[abstract / synthesis only]` (alongside **Hsieh, C.-T., & Moretti, E., 2019**, *AEJ: Macroeconomics*, 11(2), 1–39 **`[calibrated spatial-equilibrium model result, not direct reduced-form evidence]`**). **Quantity estimated**: Tight municipal PLU regulations severely depress French housing-supply price elasticity, causing demand subsidies to capitalize into land prices rather than new housing units.
3. **Conversion**: Define a standardized structural unit as a **major national zoning liberalization increasing the housing-supply price elasticity in tense French urban areas (Zones A bis / A, notably Île-de-France) by +0.5** (permitting a **+10% long-run expansion in high-productivity metro housing and workforce share**, or ~+550,000 workers relocating to Île-de-France / top metro cores over 15 years). Given an Île-de-France productivity premium (net of sorting, per Combes et al., 2012) of ~15%–20%, reallocating 1.8% of the national workforce raises aggregate labour efficiency by **+0.27% to +0.36%**, plus **~+0.15% to +0.30%** from higher steady-state residential/commercial building capital and retail competition (Bertrand & Kramarz, 2002)—totalling **+0.45% of GDP** centrally.
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **+0.15 / +0.45 / +1.00** (% of GDP per **major national zoning liberalization raising tense-zone housing supply elasticity by +0.5**), with lag **$L = 15$ years** ($t_0 = 2$).
5. **Confidence**: **Medium** (strong French empirical evidence on agglomeration elasticities, commercial zoning barriers, and regulatory supply bottlenecks from Combes et al., 2012/2019, Bertrand & Kramarz, 2002, and Chapelle et al., 2023).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Greaney, B. (2026)** (*AEJ: Macroeconomics*, 18(2), 409–428) and **Duranton & Puga (2020, *JEP*; 2023, *Econometrica*)** show that spatial-misallocation models (like Hsieh & Moretti, 2019) substantially overstate aggregate GDP gains when land rents are consumed locally and urban congestion costs rise steeply with city size.

### 14. Removing or relaxing rent control

1. **Mechanism**: Eliminating second-generation rent caps (*encadrement des loyers* in Paris, Lille, Lyon, Bordeaux, Montpellier, etc., under the 2018 ELAN law) prevents landlord withdrawal of rental units into owner-occupation or short-term rentals, encourages property maintenance, and reduces tenant residential lock-in.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Chapelle, G., & Fack, G. (2026).** *L'encadrement des loyers : effets économiques et redistributifs*. Rapport de la mission d'évaluation remis au Premier ministre et au Parlement (avec l'appui de l'IGF et de l'IGEDD, projet CASD RENTCONT), mai 2026 (synthesized in Cour des comptes, *L'encadrement des loyers*, octobre 2026, [ccomptes.fr](https://www.ccomptes.fr/)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (evaluation of the ELAN experimental rent-cap scheme across regulated municipalities), DiD on rental listings and administrative records. **Quantity estimated**: Moderated cumulative rent increases by **2% to 4%** over 2.5 years relative to counterfactual, while emphasizing short observation horizons (4–5 years), spatial spillovers, and weak redistributive targeting in Paris.
   - **Breuillé, M., Grivault, C., Le Gallo, J., Morin, Y., & Regnaud, M. (2024; updated 2025).** *Les effets de l'encadrement des loyers à Paris — Évaluation d'impact du dispositif (et extension à 5 autres villes régulées)*. Rapport APUR / CESAER / LéP, avril 2024 & juin 2025 ([apur.org](https://www.apur.org/)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (Paris and 5 regulated cities, 2018–2025), causal DiD on SeLoger listing data and OLAP lease records. **Quantity estimated**: Reduced advertised rents by **-5.0% in Paris** (-5.16% across the 6 cities) between July 2019 and June 2025, with **no statistically significant reduction in private rental housing supply** over 2018–2025.
   - **Diamond, R., McQuade, T., & Qian, F. (2019).** "The Effects of Rent Control Expansion on Tenants, Landlords, and Inequality: Evidence from San Francisco." *American Economic Review*, 109(9), 3365–3394. DOI: [10.1257/aer.20181289](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20181289) (paired with **Autor, D. H., Palmer, C. J., & Pathak, P. A., 2014**, *Journal of Political Economy*, 122(3), 661–717 on Cambridge rent decontrol) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: US quasi-experimental DiD on parcel and address-level migration data. **Quantity estimated**: San Francisco rent control reduced tenant mobility by **20%** and shrank treated rental supply by **15%** via condo conversions; Cambridge decontrol boosted building maintenance and neighbourhood property values.
3. **Conversion**: France's ELAN *encadrement des loyers* applies to 72 communes covering **~4%–5% of the total national housing stock** and caps new-lease rents at $1.20 \times$ the local median reference rent while allowing *compléments de loyer*. Direct French evaluations up to 2025–2026 (Breuillé et al., 2024/2025; Chapelle & Fack, 2026) find only a 2%–5% rent moderation and no robust short/medium-run supply contraction. Even if abolishing the ELAN regime reversed a San Francisco-scale 15% rental conversion and 20% mobility lock-in across the ~4%–5% regulated share of the national housing stock, the national job-matching / spatial-productivity gain would be at most **+0.02% to +0.05% of GDP**, with central empirical estimates indistinguishable from zero.
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **0.00 / 0.00 / +0.05** (% of GDP for **abolishing the French ELAN *encadrement des loyers* regime**), with lag **$L = 6$ years** ($t_0 = 0$).
5. **Confidence**: **Low** (no empirical study links second-generation European rent caps to aggregate GDP or employment, and French econometric evaluations up to 2026 find modest price effects without clear causal supply contraction; central $\sigma = 0.00$ recommended).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Kholodilin, K. A. (2024)** ("Rent control effects through the lens of empirical research: An almost complete review of the literature," *Journal of Housing Economics*, 63, 101983, DOI: [10.1016/j.jhe.2024.101983](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhe.2024.101983)) confirms across >100 studies that while rent control reduces private rental supply in 12 of 16 studies, its effect on *total new housing construction* is small or mixed when new builds are exempted or converted to owner-occupation.

### 15. Replacing property-transaction taxes (droits de mutation, DMTO) and the taxe foncière with a land value tax at equal yield

1. **Mechanism**: Abolishing property transfer taxes (*droits de mutation à titre onéreux*, DMTO, ~0.5% of GDP) and shifting recurrent property taxation (*taxe foncière*, TFPB, ~1.4% of GDP) off buildings/structures onto unimproved land values (Land Value Tax, LVT) eliminates both the residential/commercial mobility lock-in from transaction taxes and the tax wedge discouraging building construction and urban densification on fixed land.
2. **Best evidence**:
   - **Bonnet, O., Chapelle, G., Trannoy, A., & Wasmer, É. (2021).** "Land is back, it should be taxed, it can be taxed." *European Economic Review*, 134, 103696. DOI: [10.1016/j.euroecorev.2021.103696](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2021.103696) (paired with **Bérard, G., & Trannoy, A., 2019**, "Un impôt immobilier tout en un : rendement, progressivité et faisabilité," *Revue de l'OFCE*, 161(1), 177–217) **`[theoretical dynamic-taxation model & fiscal microsimulation, not reduced-form GDP evidence]`** `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France, secular national wealth decomposition integrated into a Judd-style optimal dynamic capital-taxation model. **Quantity analysed**: Proves theoretically that replacing taxes on structures (TFPB), transactions (DMTO), and productive capital with a single 2%–3% annual tax on unimproved land restores first-best capital accumulation and spatial density.
   - **Bérard, G., & Trannoy, A. (2018).** "The impact of the 2014 increase in the real estate transfer taxes on the French housing market / L'impact de la hausse des droits de mutation immobiliers de 2014 sur le marché du logement français." *Économie et Statistique / Economics and Statistics*, 500–501–502, 179–200. DOI: [10.24187/ecostat.2018.500t.1951](https://doi.org/10.24187/ecostat.2018.500t.1951) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: France (2014 staggered departmental hike of DMTO rates from 3.80% to 4.50%), quasi-experimental DiD across French departments on monthly notary transactions. **Quantity estimated**: Net short-run loss of **-6% of transactions over the first 3 months**, **but no statistically significant medium- or long-run effect on transaction volumes**.
   - **Best, M. C., & Kleven, H. J. (2018).** "Housing Market Responses to Transaction Taxes: Evidence From Notches and Stimulus in the U.K." *The Review of Economic Studies*, 85(1), 157–193. DOI: [10.1093/restud/rdx032](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdx032) (alongside **Banzhaf, H. S., & Lavery, N., 2010**, "Can the land tax help curb urban sprawl? Evidence from growth patterns in Pennsylvania," *Journal of Urban Economics*, 67(2), 169–179, DOI: [10.1016/j.jue.2009.08.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2009.08.005), and **Han, Ngai & Sheedy, 2025**, *REStud*, DOI: [10.1093/restud/rdae091](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdae091)) `[abstract / synthesis only]`. **Context & Method**: UK administrative property-transaction bunching/DiD and US split-rate property-tax panel DiD. **Quantity estimated**: Eliminating a 1% UK transaction tax raises transactions by **+20%** (over half permanent); Han et al. estimate a marginal deadweight loss of **87% of revenue**; Banzhaf & Lavery show shifting property tax from structures onto land significantly raises the **capital-to-land ratio and housing density**.
3. **Conversion**: The reform combines two supply channels at equal yield (~1.9% of GDP total: ~0.5% DMTO + ~1.4% TFPB):
   - *Channel A — Abolishing DMTO (~4.5%–5.8% transfer tax)*: Bérard & Trannoy (2018) find no persistent medium-run volume effect in France from a 0.7 pp change. Even using UK/Canadian elasticities for full abolition, cross-labour-market job-matching efficiency rises by at most **+0.05% to +0.15% of GDP**.
   - *Channel B — Untaxing structures in the Taxe Foncière (~0.8%–1.0% of GDP shifted from buildings onto land)*: Removing an annual ~1.0%–1.5% tax on building capital lowers the user cost of residential and commercial structures by ~12%–15%. With a long-run structure-supply elasticity to user cost of 0.25–0.50 (dampened by PLU zoning rules unless Measure 13 is simultaneously enacted) and a building-structures value-added share of ~7% of GDP, steady-state structure capital and urban density expand over 12 years to raise GDP by **+0.10% to +0.45%**.
   - *Combined effect*: **+0.10% to +0.60% of GDP** (central **+0.30%**).
4. **Proposed $\sigma$**: **+0.10 / +0.30 / +0.60** (% of GDP per **full revenue-neutral replacement of DMTO and Taxe Foncière on structures with a pure Land Value Tax**), with lag **$L = 12$ years** ($t_0 = 1$).
5. **Confidence**: **Medium** (strong international quasi-experimental evidence on transaction-tax deadweight loss from Best & Kleven, 2018 and US split-rate land taxes from Banzhaf & Lavery, 2010, tempered by Bérard & Trannoy's, 2018 null medium-run DMTO volume result in France).
6. **Known counter-evidence**: **Hilber, C. A. L., & Lyytikäinen, T. (2017)** (*Journal of Urban Economics*, 101, 70–83, DOI: [10.1016/j.jue.2017.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2017.06.002)) find in UK data that stamp duty reduces short-distance housing moves but has **no statistically significant effect on long-distance job-related mobility**; furthermore, where local zoning (PLU) strictly caps floor-area ratios, untaxing buildings cannot increase physical density and merely capitalizes into land prices.

## Part IV — Simulator Implementation Notes

1. **Avoiding Double-Counting on "TVA Sociale" (Measure 6 vs. Measures 2 & 3)**:  
   If a user constructs a "TVA sociale" by activating **Measure 6** (+1 pp VAT) alongside **Measure 2** (low-wage SSC cut) or **Measure 3** (broad SSC cut), Measure 6 must represent **only the standalone VAT-hike leg** ($\sigma_{\text{VAT-only}} \in [-0.20, 0.00]$, central $0.00$ or $-0.10$ due to SMIC/pension CPI indexation and the consumption–leisure wedge). The table entry above ($\sigma \in [-0.10, +0.20]$, central $0.00$) evaluates the **combined revenue-neutral shift** (+1 pp standard VAT financing an across-the-board payroll-tax cut).
2. **Saturation Asymmetry on Low-Wage Employer SSCs (Measure 2)**:  
   Because general URSSAF employer contributions at $1.0\text{ SMIC}$ are already virtually zero since the 2019 CICE transformation, *further cuts* ($a > 0$) face diminishing returns ($\sigma$ shifts toward Measure 3's $0.00$ beyond $1.6\text{ SMIC}$), whereas *raising* employer SSCs on low wages ($a < 0$) re-introduces a binding wedge at the SMIC and carries the full negative effect ($-\sigma = -0.45$ to $-0.80$).
3. **Complementarity Between Zoning Reform (Measure 13) and Land Value Taxation (Measure 15)**:  
   Untaxing buildings via a Land Value Tax (**Measure 15**) only generates full physical densification and construction gains if local PLU land-use rules (**Measure 13**) permit higher floor-area ratios. In an interactive simulator, the upper half of Measure 15's range ($\sigma \in [+0.30, +0.60]$) should ideally be conditioned on simultaneous activation of Measure 13.
